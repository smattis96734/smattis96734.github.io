My first instinct when thinking or even hearing about coding standards is yawning. To me it is the dullest part of coding where you style your code in a certain way to make it more readable or understandable but at the same time execute a problem in a particular way. It is like writing an essay with a template that restricts you from saying what you want. In coding there are countless ways to solve a problem and to restrict your answers because coding standards feels uncomfortable and is irritating. However, I feel that the significance of having code that is consistent and reliable outweighs the programmers personal preferences and shortcuts.

Although frustrating there are going to be situations where coding standards are necessary. For example when you work with other people having consistent code can streamline your compatibility with the software and other people. When maintaining systems readability is incredibly important. Without coding standards the process of developing, maintaining, and integrating software becomes a lot more difficult than it needs to be. In extreme cases where failure can cost a fortune or many people rely on the technology could get hurt. Having a coding standard isn't even a question. These CRITICAL software projects require bullet proof coding guidelines.

A strong example I like to remind myself of when I get irritated at coding standards is “The Power of Ten Rules for Developing Safety Critical Code”. Written by Dr. Gerard J. Holzmann who is most notable for making SPIN one of the first practical, scalable model checkers for real-world concurrent systems and has led NASA Jet Propulsion Laboratory for RELIABLE SOFTWARE. You can imagine the amount of precision that is required for such a delicate task. So when he discusses fundamental ideas of what code standards are and critiques others it has my full attention.

Holzmann right off the bat explains that coding standards lay the ground rules for software, how it should be structured, and which language features should and should not be used. Then critiques most existing guidelines for having over a hundred rules with questionable justifications. Rules like white spaces that could have been introduced by personal preferences and other rules made to prevent very specific and unlikely errors. Then Holzmann critiques that these coding standards often have very little effect on what developers actually do. Holzmann suggests that we would gain much more benefit by sticking to no more than ten rules. These rules might not cover everything but it can provide the groundwork to achieve reliable and verifiable code. He then finishes his introduction by stating “The trade-off, though, should be clear. When it really counts, especially in the development of safety critical code, it may be worth going the extra mile and living within stricter limits than may be desirable. In return, we should be able to demonstrate more convincingly that critical software will work as intended”. The following steps include coding concepts that are a little advanced for me. But even to someone that hasn’t started their career in any sort of software related positions this article makes a clear emphasis on why coding standards are important and how critical they can be.

In the end, I do not like following coding standards. I’m sure there are many other people who feel the same irritation I do when coding standards get in the way of actually solving the problem. Although this irritates me, I try to remind myself of The Power of Ten Rules for Developing Safety Critical Code and the importance of coding standards. Because personal preference shouldn’t get in the way of enhanced code especially if the code is critically important.
